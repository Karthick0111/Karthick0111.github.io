Who should you actually target?

Not the customers most likely to leave — the ones a retention offer can still change. Ranking by risk alone spends the early budget on customers already gone.

Can you trust the probability?

churn-retention-copilot
raw XGBoost isotonic-calibrated perfect

A model can rank customers correctly and still be wrong about the odds. This one was over-confident — the group it scored at 70% actually churned nearer 60%. Calibrating it pulls the curve back onto the dotted line. That mattered here because every pound of retention budget was calculated from that probability.

Case Study

Four trackers, four numbers,
one standing argument.

World Bank · mobility programme spanning 42,000 devices and 130+ country offices

The brief

Four teams — engineering, security, service management, and leadership — each kept their own compliance tracker. Every monthly review opened the same way: a debate about whose number was right, before anyone could discuss what to actually do.

What I found

Traced each tracker back to source. None of them were wrong — they were answering different questions. Different device populations, different exclusion rules, different refresh cadences. Four correct answers to four unstated questions.

What I built

One governed dataset, and a metric dictionary defining every measure: its population, its exclusions, its refresh cadence, its owner.

Underneath it, automated quality controls — completeness, duplicate, and staleness checks — so the single number could survive an audit rather than just an argument.

What I'd do differently

I wrote the dictionary after the disputes surfaced. Next time it goes first.

It is much cheaper to agree on a definition before anyone has a number they're attached to — by the time four teams have published four figures, you're not solving a data problem any more, you're negotiating.
